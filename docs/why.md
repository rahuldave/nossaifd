# Why the rules

The five sources of this site do not always agree. This page lists each disagreement, what each source
says, and what [the guide](../GUIDE.md) and [the talk guide](../TALKS.md) chose. [Sources and
credit](sources.md) says who wrote each source.

Most disagreements go away when you ask how a person takes in the text. There are three ways:

- **Reading** a long document in order, such as a report or a README. The reader can go back.
- **Skimming** many short items, such as titles, list items, notifications or the rows of a dashboard.
  The reader sees the first two words of each item.
- **Listening** to a talk. The listener hears each sentence once. Rosalind Reid says that the listener sees
  each slide for less than a minute
  ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 32](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)).

A rule that is right for one way can be wrong for another. So each choice below names the way it is for.

## Settled choices

The project's author decided these.

| Question | What the sources say | The choice |
|---|---|---|
| Where does the new fact go in a sentence? | Rosalind Reid puts it last in a written sentence and first in a spoken one ([written reports](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-written-reports.pdf), pages 5 to 7). Shankar's post puts the words that set an item apart first ([principle 2](https://fsdatalab.github.io/blog/agent-text-at-scale/#2-put-the-most-important-words-first)) | Reading: last. Listening: first. Skimming: the words that set the item apart go first |
| Where does the result go? | Rosalind Reid ends a report and a talk with the result ([tutorial deck](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf), page 16). Shankar's skill starts with the main conclusion for a busy reader | A long report keeps Reid's order and starts with a short summary of the result. A README starts with what the project does |
| Analogy, metaphor and humor | Rosalind Reid recommends them for written, visual and spoken work (tutorial deck, page 40). Shankar's skill forbids analogy and metaphor ([rule 21](https://github.com/docwriter-org/plain-writing-skill/blob/f0d3630983ac7a82aa580f1c1509d72df739ee12/skills/plain-writing/SKILL.md?plain=1#L222)) | Documents and the words on a slide stay literal. A spoken script can use them |
| Contractions | Shankar's skill allows them. The guide forbids them for readers who are not native speakers | Documents and slides: no. A spoken script: yes |
| Questions | Shankar's skill forbids several in a row. Rosalind Reid uses them as slide titles. The guide forbids them in documents | Documents: no. A talk: one question, when the next sentence answers it |
| The passive voice | Rosalind Reid says a mix of active and passive can help ([written reports](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-written-reports.pdf), page 15). ASD-STE100 asks for the active voice | Active voice, except where a passive puts the known information first |
| Items on a slide | Rosalind Reid gives 3 to 9 items as the limit of short-term memory, about how to speak (tutorial deck, page 27). The guide keeps a list to about four items | At most 4 items on a slide |
| Time for the approach | Rosalind Reid calls the approach the most important part (tutorial deck, page 25). No source gives it a share of the time | Give the approach the most care. No share of time |
| Short items read in bulk | Shankar's post gives five principles for them | A new section of the guide, rules 45 to 49, and the rules for slide titles in the talk guide |

## Open choices

The guides apply these choices now. The project's author has not yet decided them, and each one can change.

| Question | What the sources say | The choice for now |
|---|---|---|
| The length of a spoken sentence | No source gives a number for speech. The guide keeps a written sentence to 25 words | About 20 words, never more than 25 |
| Analogy on a slide | Rosalind Reid allows it in visual work. Shankar's skill forbids it on slides | A picture can make an analogy. The words on a slide stay literal |
| One structure for slide titles | Shankar's post asks for one structure in every item. Rosalind Reid mixes questions and phrases in her titles (tutorial deck, pages 13, 19 and 25) | One structure, with exceptions for the title slide, very short titles and a repeated outline slide |
| The script for one slide | Rosalind Reid says a viewer sees a slide for less than a minute (tutorial deck, page 32). No source gives a time to speak | About a minute, which is 120 words at the fastest pace |
| Where the thanks go | Rosalind Reid's deck thanks the team and the audience at the end (tutorial deck, page 39). Her presentation tips say a talk often starts with thanks ([presentation tips](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf), page 2) | Brief thanks to the hosts at the start. The team, the funders and the audience at the end |
| Numbers that no source gives | The talk checker needs exact numbers | 40 words on a slide is a hard limit, at most 2 equations on a slide, and a pace nearer 90 words a minute for a first script |
| The changes to Shankar's skill | Her skill allows "e.g.", semicolons between steps and long sentences | The guide forbids "e.g." and semicolons in prose, and it keeps a sentence to 25 words |
| Verbs with a strong feeling | Shankar's post asks for verbs such as "love" and "complain" in a dashboard. The guide removes words that add force but no fact | Allowed in short items, where the feeling is the fact. Not in prose |
| The -ing form in titles | Shankar's post writes titles of work in progress with it. The guide prefers the simple verb | Allowed in the title of work in progress. Not in prose |

## What is not checked

Three rules of ASD-STE100 come from memory of its Issue 8, because no copy of the standard was available.
The first is the active voice in procedures. The second is a limit of 20 words for an instruction and 25
for a description. The third is no -ing form as a verb. They agree with the guide. They are not yet checked against the standard.
